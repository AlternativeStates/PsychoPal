A thalamo-amygdalar circuit drives female vulnerability to chronic pain after early-life stress.

Anna Maria Borruto, Tabea Kohlund, Lukas Rettig, Martin von Renesse, Christian Alexander Hammer, Nikolas Leonhardt, Barbara Kurpiers, Claudia Calpe-López, Luca Benn, Hans-Christoph Friederich, Thomas Kuner, Rainer Spanagel, Sebastian Wieland
Science (New York, N.Y.) · 2026-11-19 · doi:10.1126/science.adx5757
AnxietyPre-clinicalPeer reviewed#chronic pain#early-life stress#amygdala

Aim Whether early-life stress and nerve injury interact to produce female-specific circuit sensitisation driving chronic pain vulnerability.

Methods Mouse study of social isolation and nerve injury, measuring synaptic gain and activity in the paraventricular thalamus to central amygdala pathway, with chemogenetic inhibition and activation.

Results Social isolation plus nerve injury amplified synaptic gain and anticipatory recruitment of the pathway in female but not male mice. Pathway inhibition rescued behaviour, while sustained or phasic activation induced hypersensitivity or avoidance.

Conclusion A latent thalamo-amygdalar synaptic gain state, unmasked by injury, may transform early-life stress into female-specific vulnerability to chronic pain and anxiety-like avoidance.

Why it matters for you: Thalamo-amygdalar circuit plasticity and sex differences in stress-related pain relate to your interest in mental health and neuroplasticity.
