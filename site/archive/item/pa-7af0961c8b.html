Human land use drives microplastic abundance in terrestrial wildlife.

Marcia de Almeida Monteiro Melo Ferraz, Arnaud L J Desbiez, Mario Henrique Alves, Justin M Calabrese, Alexandra Tiso Cumerlato, Fernanda Cristina Jacoby, Nicole Grechi, Danilo Kluyber, Erika Y Lin, Marcella Pecora Milazzotto, Grazielle Soresini, Emília Patrícia Medici, Gabriela Mastromonaco, Michael J Noonan
Nature · 2026-11-11 · doi:10.1038/s41586-026-11285-x
Peer reviewedClinical trial / evidence synthesis#microplastics#wildlife#land use

Aim To determine how human land use and the animals' proximity to development shape microplastic burdens in terrestrial wildlife.

Methods In vivo microplastic measurements in blood and tissue of free-ranging lowland tapirs and giant anteaters across Brazil, paired with high-resolution GPS tracking and human footprint exposure data.

Results Microplastic burdens increased with human footprint exposure and proximity to development and waste management infrastructure. Microplastics were also detected in individuals in seemingly pristine habitats far from development.

Conclusion Direct exposure to modified landscapes is unlikely to be the sole driver of systemic burdens. Managing proximity to development and waste infrastructure may reduce wildlife microplastic exposure.

Why it matters for you: Loosely relevant: a neuroscientist may find the systemic, non-local exposure pattern an interesting parallel to how environmental factors reach the brain.
